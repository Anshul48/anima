# Vector 5 — Creative search, archives, and finite resources

Status: bounded research evidence and proposed architectural implications. Source inspection: 2026-10-06. This note supports macro architecture; it selects no algorithm, launches no experiment, and establishes no integrated TRACE/SST capability. Three primary works were inspected. Their experiments support mechanisms within specified computational environments; the proposed organization below remains an extrapolation.

Companion architecture essay: [Vector 5 — Creative coverage](VECTOR-05-CREATIVE-COVERAGE.md).

The architectural question is how a developmental system can preserve useful alternatives and discover unexpected routes without spending its entire budget producing difference. A search process should be able to change candidates, formulations, evaluators, and the problems it attempts. It also needs to retain what makes later reuse possible. The ambition goes beyond generating more answers to an unchanged prompt, but an elaborate archive earns its place through better construction, discovery, or reuse.

## Primary evidence and inspection scope

**Mouret and Clune, *Illuminating search spaces by mapping elites* (2015).** Inspected [arXiv 1504.04909v1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.04909v1), submitted 20 April 2015; PDF dated 21 April. Read the author note, algorithm/feature definitions, and comparative results in sections 3 and 6.

MAP-Elites discretizes user-selected features and stores the highest-quality candidate found in each occupied cell. New candidates come from random initialization or variation of stored elites; feature and quality evaluations determine insertion or replacement. This produces alternatives along specified axes, rather than diversity without a representation. Resolution is chosen partly around available computation, and the map need not fill. The neural-network comparisons used equal fitness-evaluation counts; the real-arm comparison was explicitly too preliminary for reliable statistics. Crucially, the authors call this version an early draft with preliminary experimental data, with experiments to be redone. It is strong evidence for the mechanism, limited evidence for comparative superiority. [Primary preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.04909v1).

**Conti et al., *Improving Exploration in Evolution Strategies for Deep Reinforcement Learning via a Population of Novelty-Seeking Agents* (NeurIPS 2018).** Inspected the [publisher proceedings PDF](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2018/file/b1301141feffabac455e1f90a7de2054-Paper.pdf), sections 2.2–5 and Table 1; not the code or separate supplement.

Novelty is nearest-neighbor distance between behavioral characterizations and an archive. The variants optimize novelty, a fixed novelty/reward mixture, or an adaptive mixture that adds exploration after stagnation. Only iteration policies enter the archive, limiting neighbor-search overhead. Novelty helped escape deceptive traps, but novelty-only and fixed-mixture variants underperformed reward-only ES on Alien, Gravitar, and trap-free humanoid locomotion. Atari characterizations used informative game RAM during training, although policies consumed images; descriptor learning was deferred. Adaptive mixing also depended on its update schedule. The table compares roughly two billion frames for evolutionary methods with two hundred million for reported DQN/A3C results: it does not establish a matched-resource advantage over those methods. [Primary paper](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2018/file/b1301141feffabac455e1f90a7de2054-Paper.pdf).

**Wang et al., *Enhanced POET: Open-ended Reinforcement Learning through Unbounded Invention of Learning Challenges and their Solutions* (ICML 2020; PMLR 119).** Inspected the [published paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20l/wang20l.pdf), sections 3–6, and [supplement](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20l/wang20l-supp.pdf), A.7–A.8.

Enhanced POET evolves environment/agent pairs, admits challenges through a difficulty criterion, and transfers solutions between environments. Its PATA-EC descriptor compares environments through ranked, clipped scores of existing agents, replacing hand-designed environment features. This still depends on agents, scoring, and admission rules. In the original domain, the more general descriptor achieved comparable diversity/challenge but required 82.4 ± 7.31% more ES steps. Revised transfer rules reduced optimization work. On fifteen selected targets already produced and solved by POET, direct optimization and a hand-built interpolation curriculum underperformed on harder targets under the stated budgets. This demonstrates useful search organization in simulated locomotion; it does not establish general scientific discovery or indefinite progress. [Primary paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20l/wang20l.pdf).

The supplement specifies budget equivalence in simulation time steps, including direct-line ancestor rollouts and transfer attempts into those environments (A.8.2). This is target-path simulation-step matching; it does not establish a match for the full lifetime cost of the search ecosystem. A.7 also reports specialists with limited cross-environment success, qualifying any inference that a growing archive automatically yields a broadly capable individual. [Supplement](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/wang20l/wang20l-supp.pdf).

## Proposed implications for developmental search

**Keep the meanings of novelty separate.** A source can be new to the local record while containing established knowledge. A text can be unusual while recommending the same action as existing candidates. A candidate can behave differently under a probe without introducing a new scientific idea. A new formulation may reveal an old result in a useful way. Scientific novelty additionally requires comparison with relevant prior knowledge and evidence for the claimed contribution. These distinctions are proposed bookkeeping semantics, not discoveries established by the benchmark papers. A single embedding-distance score should not certify all of them.

**Treat descriptors as revisable instruments.** A descriptor chooses which differences the system can notice and which it merges. Proposed descriptors for research might include causal assumptions, predicted observations, construction constraints, failure conditions, or methods required for realization. Their utility depends on the task. Semantic distance between paragraphs might help retrieve alternatives, but it cannot substitute for observing whether candidate methods make different predictions or accomplish different work. Retaining several views can help expose blind spots; it also increases evaluation and maintenance costs. No universal descriptor is selected here.

Changing a descriptor changes archive occupancy and apparent novelty. Changing an evaluator can change which candidate deserves attention. A developmental architecture should retain enough versioned context to explain those changes, including the evaluated artifact, probe conditions, uncertainty, and evaluation procedure. It may reinterpret old observations through a new view, but should not overwrite the original basis of selection. Joint evolution of candidates and evaluators needs an external route for detecting convenient self-scoring. Otherwise, apparent progress can result from making success easier to declare.

**Separate a search archive from an evidence record.** An archive can prioritize candidates useful for reproduction, recombination, or future comparison. An epistemic record preserves what was attempted and what the observations justify. Removing a candidate from active search should not erase a consequential failure; retaining it should not endorse its explanation. Conversely, storing every token and every candidate at equal retrieval priority can make the system less usable. The proposed arrangement preserves consequential evidence and lineage while allowing search-facing views to compress, retire, or reorganize candidates according to current purpose.

This fits the current division of responsibility: [SST](../../../context/tracks/SST.md) organizes attributable alternatives and bounded search, while [TRACE](../../../context/tracks/TRACE.md) maintains episodes, interpretations, contradictions, revisions, and justified conclusions. An external domain process must still establish what counts as an informative or successful outcome. These are architectural correspondences to the current briefs, not a claim that either runtime already implements the proposed creative-search machinery. They do not require a new universal substrate before narrower search becomes useful.

**Make serendipity and failure reusable without celebrating every failure.** A method that disappoints on one objective may later suit another. Proposed retention can distinguish a transferable tool, an informative counterexample, an unresolved hypothesis, and a contradicted interpretation. The retrieval question is what conditions would make an item worth reconsidering, not whether it once had a high novelty score. Failure explanations must separate candidate defects from evaluator defects, unavailable information, inappropriate assumptions, and execution faults. A failed test can invalidate a narrow claim while leaving a method or question valuable. It cannot by itself validate a favored story about why the failure occurred.

Serendipity also needs opportunities to encounter another problem. A possible architecture permits bounded cross-task proposals and records their subsequent outcomes. Most proposals may be unhelpful. Their continued allocation should depend on useful transfer after including the cost of proposing, adapting, and checking them. An archive that produces many occupied categories but few usable descendants has preserved variation; whether it has preserved developmental capability remains unresolved.

**Budget the entire loop.** Proposed resource accounting includes generation, evaluator calls, descriptor extraction, similarity queries, archive maintenance, adaptation, revalidation, and the attention required from people. A cheap generator with expensive evaluation has a different search opportunity than the reverse. Parallel execution changes elapsed time and total expenditure differently. Larger archives can increase access and comparison work even when the next candidate costs the same to generate. Approximate indexing, selective probes, and staged evaluation are possible responses, with their own risk of overlooking useful differences; none is selected here.

A stalled objective is a reason to diagnose the loop. It may warrant exploring more, improving the evaluator, changing the formulation, supplying missing information, or stopping an unproductive branch. There is no proposed fixed novelty quota or universal stagnation threshold. A developmental system should be allowed to revise its allocation as evidence changes, while preserving what prompted the revision. Finite budgets require an account of the value forgone when one branch receives attention.

**Assess retained capability, including later reuse.** Candidate count, occupied cells, and descriptor spread describe search activity. A stronger proposed assessment asks whether retained artifacts help solve later problems, reduce required work, provide reliable counterexamples, or enable constructions that a simpler process fails to achieve. Reuse may reside across several specialists rather than in one generalist. That possibility requires evaluating access, coordination, and adaptation, not assuming that possessing many artifacts makes their competence available.

The fair simpler alternative deserves equal opportunities: ordinary objective search with restarts and the same retrieval/evaluation resources may suffice. A useful comparison would disclose total resource accounting, descriptor engineering, tuning, unsuccessful runs, and the cost of retention. Evaluation on problems not chosen solely because the archive solved them would strengthen transfer claims. Matching evaluator calls is informative when evaluation dominates costs; it is not automatically a match for every other expense. Discovery, adaptation, maintenance, and deployment benefits should remain distinguishable.

## Bounded questions for a later research brief

1. **Which differences predict useful alternatives?** Compare proposed textual, mechanistic, and outcome descriptors on a bounded task family. Ask which differences lead to distinguishable predictions or usable methods, and record the cost of extracting them. This would investigate descriptor utility, not certify creativity through distance.
2. **Which failures deserve accessible retention?** Examine whether retaining counterexamples, methods, and conditions for reconsideration helps later tasks more than retaining only successful candidates or an undifferentiated history. Preserve failures in all comparisons; vary how they become available to search.
3. **When does branching repay its cost?** Compare a proposed archive/transfer organization with a simpler search process under explicitly accounted resources and later reuse opportunities. Include unsuccessful transfer and maintenance costs. These are research questions, not launched experiments, fixed acceptance thresholds, or programme commitments.

## Access and claim boundaries

The selected papers were retrieved from author-submitted arXiv or official proceedings. The original 2011 novelty-search paper could not be retrieved through the attempted author/publisher links; its maze results are not treated as independently inspected evidence here. An unavailable NeurIPS URL was replaced by the publisher's working papers.nips.cc PDF. No software, media, reproduction, private corpus, or archive was accessed. Current named workspace briefs were used; no memory registry was consulted for this task. The survey supports concrete mechanisms and objections to simplistic diversity prescriptions. It provides no warrant that a benchmark algorithm guarantees breakthroughs, sustains open-ended scientific progress, or scales productively to trillions of tokens.
